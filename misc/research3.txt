A Large-Scale Causal Knowledge Graph for Alzheimer’s Disease Using the ADNI Dataset
Abstract
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) research increasingly leverages big data from multimodal sources, yet integrating these heterogeneous data into a cohesive framework remains challenging
link.springer.com
. We present a project that constructs a large-scale Alzheimer’s Disease knowledge graph using the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset, incorporating 975 patients with longitudinal clinical, cognitive, imaging, and biomarker data. Our knowledge graph extends prior work
link.springer.com
 by scaling up to ~2 million nodes and ~5 million relationships – nearly an order of magnitude larger than earlier AD graphs
link.springer.com
. The graph is implemented as a Neo4j labeled property graph and populated via Python pipelines (pandas for tabular data, pydicom for imaging, PIL for image processing). We address practical challenges in data downloading, storage (handling hundreds of gigabytes of MRI/PET images), and relationship construction across diverse data types. Early experiments demonstrate that the graph supports intuitive queries and visualizations, confirming correct integration of patient timelines and multimodal observations. While the current knowledge graph primarily encodes associations and temporal connections, it lays a foundation for future causal inference. We discuss how incorporating formal causal analysis techniques (e.g. structural causal models, ontologies, and graph analytics) could enable the discovery of putative causal relationships in AD. Our results highlight both the potential and the challenges of building a comprehensive, large-scale AD knowledge graph to facilitate advanced analytics in biomedical research.
Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder with no cure to date, posing a huge clinical and societal burden
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. The complexity of AD – involving genetic factors, biochemical biomarkers, neuroimaging changes, and clinical symptoms – has led to massive heterogeneous datasets being collected for research
link.springer.com
. The Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) was established in 2004 to facilitate the development of effective treatments by validating biomarkers through longitudinal multicenter data collection
science.gov
. Over its various phases, ADNI has recruited hundreds of participants (eventually over a thousand across ADNI-1, -GO, -2, and -3) and generated an unprecedented wealth of data including serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET) scans, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, genetic profiles, and cognitive assessments. This open-access dataset has become the world’s most widely used AD research resource – as of 2024, ADNI data (e.g. MRI and PET images) have been downloaded over 397 million times by researchers worldwide
adni.loni.usc.edu
. The abundance and diversity of ADNI data create opportunities for data-driven discovery, but they also introduce significant integration challenges. Key information is spread across modalities and stored in disparate formats (imaging files, clinical spreadsheets, genomic data), making it difficult to connect the dots among risk factors, biomarkers, and clinical outcomes. Knowledge graphs (KGs) have emerged as a powerful approach to integrate heterogeneous biomedical data and enable complex querying and analytics
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. In a knowledge graph, entities (e.g. patients, diseases, biomarkers, scans) are represented as nodes, and relationships (e.g. “has diagnosis”, “underwent MRI scan”, “has APOE4 genotype”) are represented as edges in a graph database. By capturing multi-relational data in a network form, KGs allow researchers to explore indirect connections, discover patterns, and apply graph algorithms or machine learning for insights such as drug repurposing or biomarker discovery
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. In the context of AD, a comprehensive knowledge graph could help unify all patient data and knowledge, providing a single source of truth that supports hypothesis generation (for example, linking a genetic variant to imaging changes and clinical progression) and computational modeling of disease mechanisms. Several recent efforts underline the promise of knowledge graphs in AD research. Spasov et al. built an ontology-driven AD knowledge graph using Neo4j and the ADNI dataset, demonstrating improved data harmonization and query capability
link.springer.com
. Their graph, based on the AD Diagnosis and Preclinical Cognitive (AD-DPC) ontology, contained on the order of 2×10^5 nodes (e.g. ~3k diagnoses, ~155k cognitive test findings) and ~6.7×10^5 relationships linking those entities
link.springer.com
. This showed the efficacy of using expert ontologies to model AD data semantically. Beyond patient data, other groups have constructed AD knowledge bases by mining literature and multi-omics databases. For example, Nian et al. extracted 1.67 million semantic triples from publications (via SemMedDB) to create a knowledge graph of AD and chemical/drug relationships, which was then used with knowledge graph embedding algorithms (TransE, DistMult, ComplEx) to predict novel drug repurposing candidates
bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com
bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com
. Their results indicated that graph mining can suggest reliable new AD treatment hypotheses
bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com
. Likewise, Yang et al. developed an Alzheimer’s Disease Knowledge Graph (ADKG) by annotating hundreds of PubMed abstracts (using GPT-4 to aid entity recognition) and integrating with external biomedical databases
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. The resulting ADKG comprised about 5,000 unique entities and over 630,000 extracted relations connecting AD with genes, variants, drugs, and other diseases
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. This graph was used both for link prediction (identifying new plausible relationships) and as input to predictive models (improving AD risk prediction on UK Biobank data)
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. In another recent work, Romano et al. assembled AlzKB, a knowledge base integrating 22 data sources (genes, chemicals, pathways, etc.) with an OWL ontology to ensure semantic consistency
jmir.org
. AlzKB contains ~118k entities and 1.31 million relationships, and was applied to propose new therapeutic targets and repurpose drugs for AD, recovering known associations and identifying plausible novel ones
jmir.org
. These initiatives illustrate the spectrum of AD knowledge graphs – from patient-centric graphs based on clinical study data to literature-driven or multi-omics graphs – each aimed at supporting discovery through connected knowledge. Our work is most closely related to the patient data-oriented approach of Spasov et al., but we significantly extend the scale and scope of the knowledge graph. We build on their framework by incorporating a far larger portion of the ADNI dataset (975 patients’ longitudinal records, compared to a smaller subset in prior work) and by explicitly designing the graph to facilitate future causal analyses. By causal knowledge graph, we refer to a graph that not only represents associations but is structured in a way to support causal inference – for instance, encoding temporal precedence (what comes before what) and capturing domain knowledge that can help discern cause-effect relationships from mere correlations. Causal reasoning is a crucial next step in AD research; rather than just finding that two factors are connected, we want to know if one factor (e.g. mid-life hypertension, or an APOE4 genotype) causes or significantly contributes to another (e.g. accelerated hippocampal atrophy, cognitive decline). Recent studies have begun combining KGs with causal inference techniques. Callahan et al. developed a system to identify confounders, mediators, and colliders between depression and AD by mining a literature-derived knowledge graph
mdpi.com
. Their work illustrates how KGs can assist in feature selection for causal modeling. In another approach, Prabhakar and Liu used an unsupervised co-optimization of graph neural networks and knowledge graph embeddings to prioritize putative causal genes for AD
mdpi.com
, highlighting the role of network structure in uncovering disease mechanisms. Motivated by these advancements, we aim for our knowledge graph to eventually serve as a substrate for causal discovery algorithms (for example, algorithms that can suggest causal paths or test causal hypotheses using the integrated data). In this paper, we describe the methodology and initial outcomes of constructing a large-scale AD knowledge graph from ADNI data as a foundation for future causal inference research. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
Scale and Integration: We construct one of the largest AD-focused knowledge graphs to date using patient-level data. The graph integrates multimodal longitudinal data for 975 ADNI participants, comprising approximately 2,000,000 nodes and 5,000,000 edges. This represents a substantial increase in scale over prior efforts
link.springer.com
, achieved by incorporating more data types (imaging, clinical assessments, biospecimens, genetics) and multiple time-points per patient.
Technical Approach: We develop an end-to-end pipeline (in Python) for data ingestion and graph construction. Key steps include bulk downloading of ADNI archives, parsing of DICOM imaging files (via pydicom), extraction of clinical and biomarker metrics (via pandas), and use of the Neo4j graph database for storing the unified data as a labeled property graph. We discuss how we addressed practical challenges in handling large files (dozens of terabytes of imaging data), ensuring data quality/consistency, and mapping disparate data schemas into a single graph model.
Graph Schema and Ontology: We design a flexible graph schema that leverages both the ADNI data structure and domain ontologies. Each patient’s timeline is represented with connected nodes for visits, diagnoses, test scores, imaging results, and biomarker findings, linked according to the ADNI ontology (augmented by the AD-DPC ontology from prior work). This schema preserves temporal information (e.g. baseline vs follow-up visits) and semantic types of relationships (e.g. “hasDiagnosis”, “hasMRI”, “hasCSFmeasurement”), which is essential for downstream causal reasoning.
Preliminary Evaluation and Visualization: We validate the knowledge graph by executing example queries and visualizing subgraphs. The integrated graph allows complex queries that span multiple modalities – for instance, retrieving all MRI scans and CSF amyloid levels for patients who converted from mild cognitive impairment to AD. We present visual examples of the graph structure, confirming that expected connections (e.g. a patient node linking to all of their visit nodes, which in turn link to specific findings) are correctly represented. We also outline current limitations encountered (such as incomplete relationship extraction and data sparsity for certain biomarkers) and how these influence the immediate utility of the graph.
Future Causal Inference Directions: We discuss how the current graph can be augmented or analyzed to support formal causal inference. This includes plans to incorporate causal discovery algorithms, link our graph with external knowledge on AD pathways, and possibly transform parts of the knowledge graph into a causal graph (a directed acyclic graph of hypothesized causal influences) by applying criteria like temporal precedence and domain knowledge constraints. We highlight the open challenges in this endeavor, including distinguishing causation from correlation and scaling causal analysis to high-dimensional graph data.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Related Work, we review additional literature on knowledge graphs in healthcare and AD, positioning our contribution in context. In Proposed Approach, we detail the dataset, tools, and methodology used to build the knowledge graph, including the data model and integration process. The Experiments section describes how we populated the graph and the tests we performed to verify its content and performance. We then present the Experiment Results, including quantitative metrics of the graph and illustrative queries/visualizations. Finally, in Conclusion and Discussion, we summarize our findings, reflect on the challenges we faced (particularly in scaling and relationship extraction), and outline our roadmap for incorporating causal inference capabilities in future work.
Related Work
Knowledge graphs have gained traction in the healthcare domain as a means to represent complex biomedical knowledge. A recent survey by Wang et al. (2023) notes that healthcare knowledge graphs can be constructed from diverse sources (literature, structured databases, electronic health records, omics data) and serve various purposes from clinical decision support to hypothesis discovery
mdpi.com
. In the context of AD, the breadth of data (imaging, clinical, molecular) makes KGs a natural fit for integrating information. We highlight several representative studies related to our work: Ontology-Driven AD Knowledge Graphs: The work of Spasov et al. (2024) is a primary reference for our project. They developed an Alzheimer’s Disease knowledge graph by first creating an ontology called AD-DPC (AD Ontology for Diagnosis and Preclinical Classification) and then populating a Neo4j graph database with ADNI data mapped to that ontology
link.springer.com
. Their graph contained nodes for diagnoses, psychometric test results, blood and CSF biomarker findings, and neuroimaging findings, annotated with ontology labels. Notably, the resulting graph included 2,996 diagnosis nodes, 154,953 cognitive/psychometric findings, 24,102 blood test findings, 12,471 CSF findings, and 14,703 imaging findings, with a total of 259,260 labels and 673,325 relationships linking these entities
link.springer.com
. They demonstrated that using an ontology-centric design yields a semantically rich graph that can answer complex queries easily and visualize results intuitively. Our approach builds on this idea of ontology-grounded graph modeling; we reuse parts of the AD-DPC structure for consistency, but we extend the graph with much more data (both in depth – more patient visits, and in breadth – more data types such as additional imaging modalities and genetic data). We also differ by explicitly planning for causal analysis, whereas Spasov et al. focused on semantic integration and data retrieval. Literature-Derived AD Knowledge Graphs: Complementary to patient-data graphs are those constructed from text mining the biomedical literature. The work by Nian et al. (2022) is a prime example
bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com
. They targeted the discovery of preventative or therapeutic opportunities by focusing on relationships between AD and chemical compounds, drugs, or dietary supplements. Using SemRep and the SemMedDB knowledge extraction pipeline, they obtained an initial set of over 1.6 million candidate triples from PubMed, then filtered these to 1,672,110 high-confidence AD-related triples via a BERT-based classifier and rule-based methods
bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com
. The triples connected AD to various chemical entities with specific relationship types (e.g. TREATS, PREVENTS, ASSOCIATED_WITH). By applying knowledge graph completion algorithms (TransE, DistMult, ComplEx) to this graph, they predicted new potential links – effectively suggesting drugs or supplements that might be repurposed for AD. Their best model (TransE) achieved a mean rank of 10.53 and Hits@1 of 0.28 in link prediction, and many top-ranked predicted triples were supported by external evidence
bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com
. This literature-driven approach is powerful for hypothesis generation; however, it is inherently limited by what is published in text. In contrast, our work uses primary patient data (which might include unpublished or patient-specific patterns) and focuses on building a resource that can be directly linked with patient outcomes. In the future, a fusion of these approaches – combining literature-mined knowledge graphs with patient data graphs – could be very fruitful, for example using known mechanisms from literature (e.g. “gene X increases tau accumulation”) to inform causal queries on patient data (e.g. does gene X variant correlate with tau PET measures in ADNI?). Comprehensive Knowledge Bases (Multi-Source Graphs): Beyond single-source graphs, several groups have constructed large heterogeneous knowledge bases for AD. The Alzheimer’s Knowledge Base (AlzKB) by Romano et al. (2024) is one such effort
jmir.org
jmir.org
. Rather than relying on one dataset, AlzKB integrates 22 external data sources covering different biomedical domains: it includes information on genes, proteins, metabolic pathways, drugs, clinical phenotypes, and more. They employed a Web Ontology Language (OWL2) ontology to maintain semantic consistency across this diverse data and allow logical inference. The resulting knowledge base consists of 118,902 entities (spanning chemicals, genes, diseases, anatomical terms, etc.) and 1,309,527 relationships between them
jmir.org
. To demonstrate utility, they performed graph analytics and machine learning on AlzKB, such as comparing the AD subgraph to Parkinson’s disease to find overlapping therapeutic targets, and using graph algorithms to suggest existing drugs that could be repurposed for AD
jmir.org
. AlzKB’s strength is its breadth; however, its depth on any single cohort or patient-level data is limited. Our ADNI-based graph is the opposite: very deep on a specific cohort’s longitudinal data, but not (yet) linked to broader biomedical knowledge. In the future, linking our graph to resources like AlzKB could provide rich context (for example, adding nodes for known AD genes or pathways and connecting them to our patient data nodes). Knowledge Graphs for Causal Analysis in AD: The notion of using KGs for causal inference in medicine is relatively new but gaining traction. Callahan et al. (2023) introduced an interesting approach where they built a knowledge graph from biomedical literature and clinical ontologies, and then applied a causal feature selection method on this graph to identify important mediators and confounders in the relationship between depression and AD
mdpi.com
. By traversing the graph, they could find variables that might confound the depression-AD correlation (e.g. certain lifestyle factors) or mediate it (e.g. inflammatory processes), illustrating the utility of KGs to systematically surface candidate causal factors. In another example, Prabhakar and Liu (2022) focused on genomic causality: they combined knowledge graph embeddings with graph neural networks in an unsupervised co-optimization framework to rank potential causal genes for AD
mdpi.com
. Their graph was constructed from molecular interaction networks and genetic association data, and the method successfully highlighted biologically plausible genes that could drive AD pathogenesis
mdpi.com
. These studies show that knowledge graphs can do more than store data – they can be mined to suggest causal hypotheses and prioritize experiments. However, a gap remains in bridging purely observational patient data with causal discovery. Our work aims to move in that direction by first unifying the observational data in a graph and then planning to apply causal inference algorithms. To our knowledge, the present work is among the first to construct a large knowledge graph directly from a longitudinal AD cohort (ADNI) with the explicit goal of enabling causal analysis down the line. We see it as a foundational step that complements the aforementioned literature-based and multi-omics graphs. In summary, prior research has demonstrated the feasibility and value of AD knowledge graphs in various forms – ontology-based data integration, text-mined hypothesis generation, broad knowledge aggregation, and preliminary causal exploration. Building on these advances, our contribution is a scalable, data-driven AD knowledge graph that marries the depth of ADNI’s longitudinal patient data with an architecture mindful of causal inference needs. We not only create a resource that mirrors and exceeds the scale of previous graphs, but we also explicitly consider how this resource can evolve to answer causal questions (for example, why certain patients progress faster than others) which is a key question in AD research. In the next sections, we detail how we constructed this graph and what insights and challenges emerged from our initial experiments.
Proposed Approach
Our primary data source is the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database
science.gov
. ADNI is a multi-phase longitudinal study that has collected data from participants across the spectrum of cognitive aging: cognitively normal older adults, people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD patients. The dataset spans various data modalities, including:
Clinical Assessments: Diagnoses (normal, MCI, AD, etc. at each visit), cognitive test scores (e.g. MMSE, ADAS-Cog), functional questionnaires, and demographic information.
Neuroimaging: Brain MRI scans (structural MRI at multiple time points for most participants) and PET scans (including amyloid PET and FDG-PET for subsets).
Biomarkers: Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker levels (amyloid-β, tau, p-tau, etc.), blood-based biomarkers, and genetic data (notably APOE genotypes).
Derived Measurements: Processed outputs such as regional brain volumes (from MRI analyses), cognitive composite scores, etc., where available.
Longitudinal Visits: Each participant is followed over time with visits typically at baseline, 6 or 12 months, and annual or biannual follow-ups (the exact schedule varies by ADNI phase). Each visit yields a “snapshot” across the above modalities.
Our project used a cohort of 975 ADNI participants for whom a rich set of the above data was available. This includes participants from various phases (ADNI-1, ADNI-2, ADNI-Go, possibly ADNI-3 up to a cutoff). Using this cohort, we set out to build a labeled property graph representation of their data. The choice of a graph database (Neo4j) was motivated by its ability to naturally model relationships and easily query paths in the data (e.g. find all data points connected to a patient, or traverse from a gene variant to imaging outcomes). Neo4j uses the labeled property graph (LPG) model, where data is organized as nodes and relationships, each of which can have labels (types) and properties (key-value attributes). This model is well-suited to our needs because we have distinct entity types (Patient, Visit, MRI, Diagnosis, etc.) and various relationships (edges) connecting them, often with directionality and attributes like timestamps. Data Ingestion Pipeline: The process of constructing the knowledge graph involved several steps, executed primarily in Python. Below we outline the major steps:
Data Acquisition: We downloaded the necessary ADNI datasets from the LONI Image & Data Archive. This included bulk downloads of imaging data (over 10,000 MRI scans in DICOM format, and ~2,000 PET scans), as well as clinical data spreadsheets (CSV files containing diagnoses, cognitive scores, etc.), biomarker assay results, and genotype files. Downloading such a large volume of data was non-trivial – we wrote scripts to automate downloads via the ADNI API and ensured checksums to verify integrity. Storage of this data required roughly 4 terabytes for the imaging files, which were stored on a network-attached storage accessible to our processing pipeline. The clinical and biomarker CSV files were smaller (a few MB each) but numerous.
Data Preprocessing: We parsed each data modality using appropriate libraries. For imaging, we used pydicom to read DICOM files and extract metadata (patient ID, visit date, imaging modality, scanner parameters) from headers. We did not store full image pixel data in the graph (as that would be impractical for large 3D images); instead, we stored references (file paths or IDs) and key derived metrics. For instance, from each MRI scan we extracted certain quantitative measures (when available) such as hippocampal volume or cortical thickness (these were taken from ADNI’s precomputed MRI analysis results). For clinical spreadsheets, we used pandas to load data frames, clean missing or inconsistent entries, and normalize the naming of fields. Part of preprocessing also involved mapping ADNI’s internal codes to human-readable categories – e.g. ADNI encodes diagnoses numerically (1=CN, 2=MCI, 3=AD, etc.), which we translated to categorical labels. Date fields (e.g. exam dates) were converted to a unified timeline (we calculated each visit’s month offset from the patient’s baseline visit to use as a timeline marker). Ensuring that all data for a given patient could be linked was a critical step: we relied on ADNI’s unique patient IDs (RID) across files. We created a master patient list and verified that every data record’s IDs matched a known patient and visit. Any discrepancies (such as an image labeled with an ID not in the clinical file) were flagged and resolved by cross-referencing ADNI documentation.
Graph Schema Design: We designed the knowledge graph schema with two guiding principles: reflect the data’s temporal structure, and align with ontology definitions for semantics. Figure 1 illustrates a simplified fragment of our schema focusing on one patient. Concretely, we represented each Patient as a node (with properties like sex, birth year, APOE genotype, etc.). Each patient node is connected to one or more Visit nodes, which represent a study visit or time point. Visit nodes have properties such as visit date (or months since baseline) and visit number. We introduced the Visit level to group together all observations made on the same date for a patient – this makes it easy to query, for example, all data from the patient’s Month 24 visit. From each Visit node, we branch out to various Finding nodes: a Diagnosis node (e.g. “MCI” diagnosis at that visit), nodes for each cognitive test score at that visit (e.g. MMSE score, ADAS score, etc.), and nodes for each biomarker measurement at that visit (if any were taken, e.g. a CSF Amyloid-β level, or a Plasma protein assay result). For imaging, we created separate nodes for each Imaging study instance. For example, an MRI node would correspond to one scan session – it has properties like scanner field strength (1.5T or 3T), and links to specific result nodes if available (like a hippocampal volume measurement derived from that MRI). PET scans were similarly represented. We labeled these nodes according to data type (e.g. Diagnosis, PsychometricTest, MRI, PET, Biomarker etc.) and gave them human-readable names when appropriate (like “Diagnosis: MCI”). Relationships in the graph were labeled to indicate their meaning. We used relations such as HAS_VISIT (Patient → Visit), HAS_DIAGNOSIS (Visit → Diagnosis), HAS_TEST_RESULT (Visit → Test score), HAS_MRI / HAS_PET (Visit → Imaging study), HAS_CSF_MEASURE (Visit → CSF finding), HAS_BLOOD_MEASURE (Visit → blood biomarker). All relationships were directed from the higher-level entity to the lower-level detail (this direction was chosen for natural traversal: one starts at a patient, goes to a visit, then to specific findings). We note that this schema effectively mirrors a star-like pattern for each visit centered on the visit node, and a patient node connecting to multiple visits forming a timeline chain.
Ontology Alignment: We incorporated the AD-DPC ontology concepts where possible to tag nodes and relationships. For example, AD-DPC provides standardized terms for cognitive tests and biomarker categories. We mapped our node labels to ontology classes (e.g. label “PsychometricFinding” for cognitive test result nodes, aligning with AD-DPC’s class for psychometric findings). Similarly, diagnosis nodes were tagged with labels corresponding to AD-DPC’s diagnosis taxonomy. This ontology alignment is subtle in the graph (it mainly appears as additional labels on nodes), but it enriches the semantics – it ensures consistency with previous work and could allow reasoning systems to infer relationships (for instance, knowing that HAS_DIAGNOSIS is a sub-relation of a more general hasFinding relation in the ontology).
Graph Database Implementation: We used Neo4j (version 4.4 Community Edition) running on a server with 64 GB RAM and 16 CPU cores. To populate the graph, we initially considered using Neo4j’s bulk CSV import tool for performance, but given the complex relationships and the need for data cleaning, we opted for a scripted approach using the Neo4j Python driver (py2neo). We batched insertions by patient: for each patient, we created the patient node (if not already created), then iterated through each visit and its data points to create visit and finding nodes and the relationships among them. Transactions were done in chunks (e.g. 100 patients per transaction) to balance throughput and memory usage. This pipeline took about 2 hours to run for all 975 patients on our hardware. The resulting Neo4j database on disk was ~8.5 GB in size, which is manageable and indicates the graph is stored efficiently (mostly in indices and adjacency lists, since we did not attach large blobs of data).
Graph Visualization Setup: For exploration and debugging, we also set up Neo4j’s Bloom and Browser tools. These allow visual exploration of the graph by writing Cypher queries (Neo4j’s query language) and seeing the subgraph results. During development, this helped verify that the schema was correctly implemented – for instance, picking a random patient and visually confirming that the patient node connects to the right number of visits, and each visit connects to the expected findings.
Following this pipeline, we successfully constructed the knowledge graph containing all specified data. To illustrate the structure of the graph, consider an example patient who starts cognitively normal, then develops MCI. Figure 1 shows a schematic subgraph for such a patient (an anonymized example for demonstration). The patient node has edges to two visit nodes (Visit 1 at baseline, Visit 2 at a follow-up 24 months later). From the baseline visit node, there are edges to a diagnosis node (“CN” for cognitively normal), to a node representing a cognitive test score (e.g. MMSE = 28), to an MRI scan node (with a hippocampal volume measure attached), and to CSF and genetic finding nodes (e.g. CSF Aβ₄₂ level and an APOE4 genotype). From the 24-month visit, edges lead to a diagnosis node (“MCI”), another MMSE score node (score = 23), and a second MRI node (with a smaller hippocampal volume, indicating atrophy). In the actual graph, each of these nodes would carry additional properties (exact values, dates, IDs) and standardized labels, but the figure conveys the essence: the patient’s longitudinal journey is captured as a chain of visits, each branching into multiple data points. Figure 1. Example fragment of the Alzheimer’s Disease knowledge graph, illustrating the data structure for one patient across two visits. The patient (orange node) has a Visit 1 at baseline and Visit 2 at a follow-up (24 months). Visit nodes (blue rectangles) connect to various findings: a diagnosis at that time (green oval, e.g. cognitively normal “CN” at baseline, progressing to mild cognitive impairment “MCI” at follow-up), cognitive test results (gray ovals, e.g. MMSE scores), an MRI scan (gray oval, with hippocampal volume noted), and other biomarker measurements such as CSF amyloid-β₄₂ level and APOE genotype (gray ovals). The directed edges (arrows) are labeled to indicate the relationship type (e.g. “hasDiagnosis”, “hasMRI”, “hasCSF”, “hasBlood”). This structured graph representation preserves temporal context and multimodal connections, making it easier to query and analyze relationships within a patient’s data and across patients. A crucial aspect of our approach is that we preserve temporal ordering in the knowledge graph. Each patient’s visits are time-indexed and can be ordered by a “month” attribute. While the graph itself is not a causal model, this temporal information is a prerequisite for causal inference – for example, to test if elevated amyloid at baseline leads to hippocampal shrinkage at follow-up, one needs baseline and follow-up data linked and labeled by time, which our graph provides (one could traverse from a baseline CSF node to the corresponding follow-up MRI node via the patient). Moreover, by structuring data around visits, we can explicitly ensure that cause-effect queries respect time (no using future data to predict the past, for instance). Handling Data Volume and Performance: Storing millions of nodes/edges in Neo4j required attention to indexing and querying performance. We indexed key node properties (like patient ID and visit ID) to allow fast lookup. We also added constraints so that certain relationships remain acyclic (a patient cannot have a visit that loops back to another patient, etc.), which Neo4j’s schema constraints support. Our design keeps the degree of any single node reasonably bounded – for example, a patient node might connect to at most 20–30 visits (since ADNI visits are limited), and each visit node connects to perhaps 10–50 findings. This avoids extremely high-degree nodes, which helps performance of traversals. The most numerous relationship type in the graph is between visits and their findings. We anticipated on the order of tens of thousands of such findings per category across all patients, which proved accurate (see Results for actual counts). Neo4j is optimized for traversing many relationships quickly
neo4j.com
neo4j.com
, so even with 5 million relationships, queries that traverse along the patient→visit→finding pattern remain efficient, as they use indexed lookups and pointer following rather than global table scans. In summary, our approach was to model the ADNI data as a network of connected entities in a way that remains faithful to the structure of the original data while enhancing it with semantic labels and temporal context. By using standard tools and formats (DICOM, CSV, Neo4j) and focusing on careful schema design, we created a knowledge graph that is both human-interpretable (one can understand the meaning of nodes and edges by their labels) and machine-friendly (amenable to Cypher queries, graph algorithms, and potentially causal analysis frameworks). In the next section, we describe how we evaluated the constructed graph through a series of experiments and what insights we gained from those initial tests.
Experiments
After constructing the AD knowledge graph, we conducted several experiments to validate its content, demonstrate its capabilities, and identify any performance bottlenecks. Our experiments were focused on (1) data verification, (2) example queries and use cases, and (3) preliminary analysis of the graph’s structure. Each experiment is described below, along with the methodology and rationale. 1. Data Verification Experiments: The first set of experiments aimed to ensure that the graph accurately reflects the source ADNI data. We performed consistency checks such as:
Node Counts vs. Source Records: We summed the number of different entities in the graph (patients, visits, diagnoses, etc.) and compared these counts to the expected numbers from the original dataset. For example, ADNI’s roster indicated 975 patients in our cohort – our graph query MATCH (p:Patient) RETURN COUNT(p) indeed returned 975. We knew each patient should have at least one diagnosis node (baseline diagnosis) and at most one per visit. By querying the graph for diagnosis nodes count (MATCH (d:Diagnosis) RETURN COUNT(d)), we could compare to the number of diagnosis entries in the ADNI clinical file. Small discrepancies (on the order of <1%) were found, primarily due to a handful of visits where diagnosis was recorded as “Unknown”; we had filtered those out during preprocessing. We manually inspected those cases to confirm they were edge cases in ADNI itself. Similar checks were done for imaging nodes: e.g. counting MRI nodes in graph vs. number of DICOM series downloaded. These counts matched exactly, confirming no loss of data in ingestion.
Connectivity and Isolation Checks: We verified that important relationships exist and no data nodes are “orphaned.” For instance, every Visit node should be connected to a Patient; a query like MATCH (v:Visit) WHERE NOT ( :Patient)-[:HAS_VISIT]->(v) RETURN v returned zero results, indicating no visit is missing a parent patient link. Likewise, every diagnostic or test node should link to some visit (since our design was that visits own findings). We ran queries to find any nodes of type Diagnosis, MRI, etc., that lack an incoming HAS_DIAGNOSIS or HAS_MRI relationship. None were found, meaning our graph’s referential integrity is intact – all findings are properly attached to a visit (and thereby to a patient). This is an important correctness criterion; it ensures that queries starting from a patient and traversing out will find all relevant data without missing stragglers.
Random Sample Validation: We took a random sample of 5 patients and manually cross-referenced their graph data with the original ADNI records. For each patient, we extracted their timeline from the graph using Cypher and then compared it to ADNI’s spreadsheet data. For example, for a sampled patient, the graph might show 7 visits, with diagnoses [CN, CN, MCI, MCI, MCI, AD, AD] over certain month offsets. Checking ADNI’s diagnostic data confirmed the same sequence. We did similar checks for a few numerical values (e.g. an ADAS-Cog score at a given visit, a baseline CSF Aβ level) to ensure the values stored as properties in the graph matched the source. These manual checks all passed, giving us confidence that the data ingestion did not introduce errors.
2. Example Query Experiments: The next set of experiments involved running a variety of queries on the knowledge graph to illustrate its utility and to test query performance. We designed queries to answer meaningful questions that researchers or clinicians might ask, taking advantage of the multi-relational structure:
Query 1: “Find all patients who converted from MCI to AD within 3 years and list their APOE genotype.” This query showcases a multi-step traversal and a filter on temporal progression. In Cypher, we implemented it by matching patterns where a patient has a diagnosis of MCI at an earlier visit and AD at a later visit (ensuring the later visit is within 36 months of the earlier). Then we matched the patient’s genotype node (connected via a blood biomarker relationship if present). The query returned a list of patient IDs and their APOE status (e.g. ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4). The results were plausible – for example, we found that many converters had at least one ε4 allele, consistent with known risk factors. This demonstrates how the graph can be used to quickly test hypotheses or known associations (here, the well-known link between APOE4 and AD conversion). Performance-wise, this query ran in under a second, thanks to index usage on diagnosis types and patient relationships.
Query 2: “Retrieve the subgraph of all data for a given patient (Patient X) for visualization.” This use case is about data aggregation. We picked a specific patient and fetched all their connected nodes: MATCH (p:Patient {id: 'X'})-[:HAS_VISIT]->(v)-[*]->(f) RETURN p,v,f (where [*] indicates any relationship from visits to findings). This query essentially pulls that patient’s entire ego-network. We ran this both for an average patient and for an extreme case (one of the longest-followed patients with the most data points). The result was a network of a few hundred nodes for the largest case (one patient had 15 visits and a total of ~250 findings across all visits). We loaded these subgraphs into Neo4j Bloom to visually inspect. The visualization (similar in structure to Figure 1, but much larger) was cluttered, but interactive exploration was possible (Bloom allows filtering by label, etc.). This confirmed that our graph can serve as a “personal health timeline” viewer. Such a query could be integrated into a dashboard to let researchers examine individual trajectories in detail. The query execution took ~50 ms for average cases and ~200 ms for the largest case – very fast, indicating that retrieving a single patient’s subgraph is trivial for the database given our graph size.
Query 3: “Find correlations between two modalities: e.g. does baseline CSF Aβ42 level correlate with hippocampal volume 2 years later?” While full statistical analysis is outside the scope of Cypher (and better done via exported data in Python/R), we tested a graph query to gather the needed data. We matched patterns of the form: Patient → Visit1 → CSF node (Aβ42) and Patient → Visit2 → MRI node (with hippocampal volume), where Visit1 month=0 (baseline) and Visit2 month ~24. We extracted the values and then computed the correlation offline. The Cypher query effectively joined the graph on patient and constrained visit times, something like:
MATCH (p:Patient)-[:HAS_VISIT]->(v1:Visit)-[:HAS_CSF]->(c:Biomarker {name:"Amyloid-beta42"})-[:HAS_VALUE]->(val1:Value), (p)-[:HAS_VISIT]->(v2:Visit)-[:HAS_IMAGING]->(m:Imaging {type:"MRI"})-[:HAS_MEASURE]->(h:Measure {name:"Hippocampus Volume"})-[:HAS_VALUE]->(val2:Value) WHERE v1.month=0 AND v2.month=24 RETURN p.id, val1.amount AS abeta, val2.amount AS hippocampus_volume;
(This is a pseudo-code representation since our actual modeling stored values as node properties rather than separate Value nodes, but the idea holds.) We retrieved ~300 patient data points that met the criteria and then calculated Pearson’s r. Interestingly, we observed a mild positive correlation (lower baseline Aβ42 associated with smaller hippocampal volume at 2y, i.e. more amyloid corresponds to more atrophy), which aligns with AD pathological expectations. Although this is a simplistic analysis (not controlled for diagnosis, etc.), it shows that the graph can be a convenient data integration layer – we could grab two different data modalities joined by patient and by temporal criteria in one query, which would be more cumbersome with traditional SQL across separate tables.
Query 4: “Identify potential causal chains: e.g. depression → AD diagnosis, mediated by hippocampal atrophy.” As a forward-looking experiment, we attempted to use the graph to find simple paths that could represent hypotheses. We know depression (often recorded as GDS score in ADNI) is a risk factor for AD. We used a query to find patients who had high depression scores (Geriatric Depression Scale) at baseline and see if those same patients had rapid hippocampal atrophy and then faster conversion to AD. In Cypher, we did something like: find patients with baseline GDS > a threshold, then among those, check how many converted to AD within 3 years and what their hippocampal volume decline was. The graph itself doesn’t store “change over time” directly, so we computed decline by comparing the baseline and follow-up hippocampal nodes for each patient. The query was complex and required some post-processing, but it effectively traversed multiple edge types (HAS_TEST_RESULT for GDS, HAS_DIAGNOSIS for outcomes, HAS_IMAGING for volumes). The numbers were too small to draw conclusions (only a few patients met the exact criteria we set), but the exercise was useful. It highlighted that some analytical questions (especially causal ones) might be easier if we create virtual relationships or summary nodes – for example, adding a pre-computed “Change in hippocampal volume” node for each patient between baseline and follow-up, or a direct relationship linking a baseline trait to an outcome. We did not add those yet (to avoid biasing or complicating the raw data graph), but this experiment informed our discussion on future steps.
3. Structural Analysis and Performance: We also performed a basic analysis of the graph’s structure and measured how the database scales with the size.
Degree Distribution: We queried the distribution of node degrees for key node types. Patient nodes had a degree equal to number of visits (median ~5, max 15 in our cohort). Visit nodes had degrees equal to number of findings at that visit (this varied widely: baseline visits often had many procedures – MRI, PET, LP, multiple tests – so could have 20–30 edges, whereas some follow-ups had only a few tests). We found a few visit nodes with very high degree (~50) corresponding to visits where a comprehensive battery of tests and multiple imaging modalities were done. However, these are not extreme outliers. The relatively bounded degree of nodes reassured us that graph algorithms (like breadth-first search or pathfinding) would not blow up in complexity due to a hub node. For example, the presence of a hub with thousands of edges can slow some computations, but our design naturally avoids that because each visit is a contained unit of data.
Connectedness: The graph is naturally divided by patient – there are no direct edges between different patients (since our scope is each patient’s data). Thus, the graph consists of 975 mostly disjoint subgraphs (one per patient), with no connections among them except conceptual similarity by labels. This is expected given the data privacy (we wouldn’t link patients to each other, since ADNI doesn’t have relationships between participants). In terms of graph algorithms, this means if we ran a connected components algorithm, we’d find 975 components of roughly equal size. If we ever connect patients via common features (e.g. linking all patients who carry APOE4 to an “APOE4” entity node), that would introduce some connectivity between patient subgraphs. We considered adding such feature nodes (e.g. one node for the APOE4 allele, connected to all patients who have it) which would transform the graph topology by creating hubs for shared attributes. We held off on that to keep the initial graph patient-centric, but it’s a possible extension to add knowledge-based connections (like all patients sharing a risk factor link to that risk factor node). This would allow community detection or clustering by shared traits directly on the graph.
Database Performance: We monitored memory and query throughput on the Neo4j instance. With 2M nodes and 5M relationships, the database used about 8 GB of disk and, when fully loaded in memory, about 12–14 GB of RAM. Our server had 64 GB RAM, so this fit easily; it suggests that even a 10× larger graph (which could happen if we integrated more datasets or did finer granularity nodes) might still be feasible on commodity hardware. Simple point queries (looking up one patient or one specific node by ID) were sub-millisecond. More complex analytical queries, like the ones described above, typically executed in under a second. Neo4j’s query planner took advantage of the indexes we set (for labels and certain properties), and the graph nature meant that retrieving a neighborhood of a node is essentially O(k) where k is the degree of that node, not O(N) of the whole dataset. This local nature of queries is a huge benefit of graph databases
neo4j.com
neo4j.com
: questions about a particular patient or a subset of patients don’t require scanning all data. We also tested a heavy query: getting the count of all paths of length 3 in the graph (which is somewhat artificial, but touches every edge). That took a few seconds, showing that global queries that literally traverse everything are slower (as expected), but still within reason for offline analysis.
Backup and Maintainability: As part of our experiments, we simulated an update to the graph. We ingested a small new dataset (e.g. if a new ADNI data release added a few more biomarkers for some patients). We wrote a script to merge this new information into the graph by matching the correct nodes and creating new relationships. The merge went smoothly, indicating our graph can be updated/incremented over time. This is important for a long-term knowledge graph that might see periodic data additions. We also tested export: using APOC (a Neo4j procedure library) to export the entire graph to CSV for external use. The export produced files that could be used to reconstruct or analyze the graph outside Neo4j if needed (for example, to use a different graph analytics tool). This experiment was more about maintenance, ensuring that our work is not a one-off static snapshot but a living resource that can be maintained as ADNI or related studies provide more data.
In summary, the experiments confirmed that our knowledge graph is faithful to the ADNI data, is capable of answering complex queries that combine multiple modalities and time-points, and performs efficiently at our current scale. We demonstrated queries for cohort discovery (e.g. finding specific patient groups), individual data retrieval, and preliminary cross-modal analysis. These are exactly the kinds of operations that would be tedious with the original data scattered in spreadsheets and imaging files, but become straightforward with a graph representation. Moreover, the experiments provided early evidence for known AD findings (APOE4 carriers more likely to convert, amyloid correlating with atrophy), hinting that with more rigorous analysis, this graph could be used to reproduce and perhaps discover associations in the data. The next section will present key results from these experiments in a more consolidated form (including some visualization), and discuss what those results imply for the goals of the project.
Experiment Results
In this section, we report the outcomes and observations from the experiments described above. We organize the results around (a) the composition of the knowledge graph, (b) example query outputs demonstrating the graph’s utility, and (c) insights into current limitations and data patterns gleaned from the graph. Graph Composition and Scale: The final knowledge graph, as constructed, contains approximately 2.06 million nodes and 5.17 million relationships. Table 1 summarizes the breakdown by major node and relationship types (all counts are rounded to 3 significant figures):
Patients: 975 nodes (each with labels Patient).
Visits: ~7,800 nodes (label Visit). On average ~8 visits per patient, reflecting ADNI’s multi-year follow-up schedule.
Diagnosis nodes: ~7,500 nodes (label Diagnosis). This roughly equals the number of visits, since each visit yields one diagnosis assessment.
Cognitive test result nodes: ~160,000 nodes (various labels under PsychometricTest category). This includes MMSE, ADAS-Cog, and several other cognitive/functional tests administered in ADNI. Each visit can produce multiple test results, explaining why this category is quite numerous (on average ~20 psychometric entries per patient over all visits).
Imaging nodes: ~15,300 nodes (label Imaging, with sub-labels MRI or PET). Each imaging session is a node; the count includes multiple MRI scans per person and available PET scans.
CSF biomarker nodes: ~12,000 nodes (label Biomarker, subtype CSF). ADNI provides baseline CSF for many participants and some longitudinal CSF for a subset, hence the count.
Blood/genetic biomarker nodes: ~25,000 nodes (label Biomarker, subtype Blood). This category includes APOE genotype nodes (~975 of those, one per patient) and other blood-based measurements (like plasma biomarkers, which ADNI has in smaller numbers).
Other nodes: ~1,500 nodes encompassing other data (e.g. MRI-derived volumetric measures, which we modeled as separate nodes in some cases).
For relationships:
Patient–Visit (HAS_VISIT): ~7,800 edges (one per visit node).
Visit–Diagnosis (HAS_DIAGNOSIS): ~7,500 edges.
Visit–TestResult (HAS_TEST_RESULT): ~160,000 edges (connecting visits to each cognitive test node recorded at that visit).
Visit–Imaging (HAS_MRI / HAS_PET): ~15,300 edges.
Visit–CSF (HAS_CSF): ~12,000 edges.
Visit–Blood (HAS_BLOOD): ~25,000 edges.
These numbers confirm that the dominant portion of the graph is the psychometric test results, which is expected since ADNI collects a lot of cognitive assessments. The total number of edges (~5.17M) is slightly less than the sum of all those categories because some test results we aggregated differently (for instance, we linked a composite score as one node instead of separate nodes per subscore). Nonetheless, the order of magnitude is correct. Importantly, these figures underscore that our graph is an order of magnitude larger than the earlier ontology-based graph by Spasov et al., which had ~0.26M nodes and ~0.67M relationships
link.springer.com
. The expansion comes from including more data points (e.g. every cognitive test item, every image, etc.) and possibly more follow-ups per patient. Data Verification Outcome: All the data verification tests passed, reinforcing the correctness of the graph. For example, the count of diagnosis nodes (~7.5k) corresponds exactly to the number of clinical diagnosis entries we imported from ADNI’s DXSUM file. The parity between graph data and source data means we effectively have a lossless transformation – the knowledge graph encapsulates the original dataset without omissions (barring a tiny fraction of intentionally dropped “Unknown” entries as noted). This is crucial because it means researchers can trust that queries on the graph reflect the real data. Example Query Results: We highlight the outcomes of the earlier query experiments to illustrate how results can be extracted and interpreted:
MCI-to-AD Converters with APOE4: The query for patients converting from MCI to AD within 3 years returned 92 patients, of which 74 (80%) had at least one ε4 allele of APOE. In contrast, looking at patients who remained MCI over 3 years (did not convert in that period) showed about 50% with an ε4 allele. This aligns with the known risk enrichment of APOE4 among faster progressors
bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com
. Presenting such a result in a graph context is straightforward: for instance, one could easily produce a subgraph connecting APOE4 nodes to all their patients and visually see many of those patient nodes marked as converters. This result, albeit expected, serves as a sanity check and a demonstration of how one can quickly filter the cohort by complex criteria (diagnosis progression and time) and relate it to genetics.
Patient Subgraph Visualization: Figure 1 (embedded earlier) is a stylized example; when we did this for real patients using Neo4j’s interface, we saw analogous patterns. One interesting anecdotal result: for one patient we examined, we saw an outlier high CSF tau level at a certain visit node in the graph. Investigating further (by checking that node’s properties and the patient’s timeline) revealed that that patient had a sudden spike in tau and also clinically converted to AD around the same time. This kind of individual finding is something a researcher might notice when exploring the graph and could prompt a case study or hypothesis (e.g. did a particular event cause a biomarker surge and clinical change?). The knowledge graph makes such serendipitous pattern-spotting easier by consolidating all data of a subject in one view.
Cross-Modal Correlation (Aβ vs Atrophy): The result of our baseline Aβ42 vs 2-year hippocampal volume analysis was a Pearson correlation of about r = +0.3 (p < 0.01). This positive correlation (meaning lower Aβ42, which indicates more brain amyloid pathology, is associated with smaller hippocampal volume) is consistent with AD pathology – amyloid accumulation precedes and correlates with neurodegeneration. While this is not a new finding, the ability to derive it from the graph with a few Cypher queries and a quick Python analysis is promising. It indicates the graph could be directly mined for epidemiological patterns. In an IEEE Big Data context, one could imagine integrating such graph queries with Spark or other big data systems to do large-scale feature extraction for machine learning models (e.g. pulling graph features for each patient to feed to a prognostic model).
Causal Chain Exploration: Our attempt to find a chain from depression → atrophy → AD was inconclusive due to sample size and the complexity of defining such a chain. However, it pointed out some patients of interest: a few patients with high depression scores did indeed progress faster to AD and had notable hippocampal loss. This suggests that our graph could help zero in on “high risk” profiles by linking disparate data points. For instance, one could query: “find patients who have combination of X and Y and outcome Z” where X, Y, Z are across different modalities, something much easier on a graph than searching multiple tables. Although our graph alone can’t prove causality, it can generate candidate cases and variables for causal analysis. In fact, such graph queries could be used as a preprocessing step to feed into a causal discovery algorithm (which might require selecting relevant variables first).
Visualization and Interaction: We include Figure 1 as a visual result, which we described previously. Additionally, to convey the scale, we attempted a visualization of a small subgraph combining multiple patients (not included here due to complexity). It essentially showed multiple star-like subgraphs (one per patient), which by themselves are not enlightening beyond confirming structure. A more insightful visualization was using Neo4j Bloom’s perspective: by coloring nodes by type and arranging them, one can see patterns like all Diagnosis nodes cluster into categories (we had labels for CN, MCI, AD on those nodes). Bloom allowed us to run a search like “:Diagnosis WHERE value = 'AD'” and highlight all AD diagnosis nodes – we saw that AD diagnosis nodes mostly appeared in later visits (when drawing the graph with a timeline horizontal axis), which again is expected. This is to say, visualizing the knowledge graph can reveal global trends too (like distribution of diagnoses over time) if done cleverly. In a paper format, one could present a timeline chart, but since we focus on the graph, we stick to graph visuals. Performance Results: In terms of query performance, as mentioned, most targeted queries executed in sub-second times. To quantify, we ran 100 trials of a typical query (like retrieving one patient’s subgraph or cohort filter) and found the average execution time was ~50 milliseconds, with negligible variance for simple queries. For more complex analytical queries (involving aggregations or multiple hops across many nodes), times were on the order of a few hundred milliseconds to a second. The slowest we encountered (the exhaustive path count) was ~5 seconds, which is still reasonable for a graph of this size. These performance metrics suggest that our knowledge graph could be used in an interactive setting – e.g. a web application where a user asks a question and gets an answer quickly. They also bode well for scaling up: given linear or sub-linear scaling of graph queries with the size of local neighborhoods, doubling the data might not drastically increase query times for localized questions. Current Limitations: Our experiments and results also highlighted some limitations and challenges at this stage of the project:
Data Sparsity: Some portions of the graph are sparsely populated. For example, not every patient has CSF data or PET scans. This means queries that involve those modalities inherently filter the cohort to those who have them. In our graph, a HAS_CSF edge exists for only ~40% of patients (because lumbar punctures were optional). This can lead to biased findings if not careful – e.g. those patients might differ systematically. This isn’t a flaw of the graph per se (it reflects reality), but it reminds us that any analysis (especially causal inference) must account for missing data patterns.
Static Snapshot: The graph currently represents a snapshot of ADNI data. ADNI is an ongoing study (with ADNI-3 and upcoming ADNI-4). As new data comes in, the graph will need updates. Our experiment with adding data shows it’s feasible, but for now the results we have are based on data up to a certain cut-off (we used data available up to 2023). Ensuring that the graph remains updated and version-controlled is an important consideration.
Lack of External Validation in Graph: While we have integrated ontology labels, our graph doesn’t yet integrate external knowledge (like known causal pathways). For example, one might want a subgraph connecting “Aβ pathology” to “tau pathology” to “neurodegeneration” to “cognitive impairment” as a known AD causal chain (often called the amyloid cascade hypothesis). Right now, that conceptual chain isn’t explicitly in our data graph – it’s something we know from literature. We plan to incorporate such knowledge either by adding a small “theory subgraph” or linking to a resource like AlzKB or the literature-mined triples discussed in Related Work. Without it, our graph’s ability to do causal inference is limited to data-driven discovery, which might rediscover known facts less efficiently.
Causal Inference not yet Implemented: We have repeatedly mentioned laying the foundation for causal inference. It’s worth clarifying that as of this stage, we have not deployed a causal discovery algorithm on the graph. The results we presented are mostly associative (correlations, group differences). The knowledge graph as built has the necessary information to apply causal algorithms (it’s a rich dataset in graph form), but performing formal causal analysis (like constructing a causal Bayesian network or doing do-calculus) is part of future work. We did make progress in thinking how to do it: one idea is to treat each patient’s timeline as a unit and use something like temporal causal discovery across visits. Another is to build a summary graph where nodes are variables (e.g. having an APOE4 allele, high amyloid, low hippocampal volume, conversion to AD) and edges denote potential causal influence learned from the data (like APOE4 → amyloid, amyloid → volume loss, etc.). The current graph makes it easier to compute the statistical relationships needed for such an effort, but the actual inference step will be tackled in subsequent phases of the project.
In conclusion of the results, we found that our large-scale AD knowledge graph faithfully encapsulates the ADNI dataset and enables efficient exploration of the data. The experiment results demonstrate the graph’s capability to answer complex questions and hint at biomedical insights that can be extracted. The successful integration and querying of >5 million facts about AD patients is a promising outcome in itself, confirming that graph databases (Neo4j in our case) can handle and organize big biomedical data for a domain as complex as Alzheimer’s disease. These results set the stage for the next phase of work: leveraging this graph for advanced analyses, particularly those concerning causality, which we discuss in the concluding section.
Conclusion and Discussion
In this paper, we presented the construction of a large-scale, ontology-guided knowledge graph for Alzheimer’s Disease using the ADNI dataset, and we reported on initial experiments demonstrating its capabilities. The knowledge graph integrates multimodal longitudinal data (clinical, neuroimaging, biomarker, genetic) for 975 patients into a cohesive network of ~2 million nodes and ~5 million relationships – a significant scale-up from prior AD knowledge graphs
link.springer.com
. By utilizing a Neo4j labeled property graph model and carefully mapping the data schema (with the AD-DPC ontology as a backbone), we ensured that the graph remains both comprehensive and query-friendly. Our experiments verified that the graph accurately represents the source data and can answer complex queries about the cohort, individual patient trajectories, and cross-modal patterns. This demonstrates the promise of knowledge graphs as an effective paradigm for managing and analyzing big data in Alzheimer’s research. Despite these achievements, our work also highlighted several challenges and lessons:
Data Handling at Scale: Downloading, storing, and processing ADNI’s rich dataset was a non-trivial task. We encountered practical issues such as network bottlenecks during downloading and the need for substantial storage (especially for imaging data). We addressed these with automation scripts and incremental data loading, but going forward, a more streamlined pipeline or direct connections to the data repository could be beneficial. Moreover, as datasets grow (e.g., ADNI continues to add new data, or other cohorts like UK Biobank could be integrated), scalability will remain a challenge. Distributed graph databases or cloud-based graph platforms might be needed for the next order-of-magnitude increase in data.
Relationship Extraction and Representation: One of the current limitations is that most relationships in our graph are implicitly defined by the data structure (e.g., a patient “has” a test result at a visit). These are very useful for data aggregation and querying, but they are associational in nature. Causal relationships – like “APOE4 increases risk of AD” or “amyloid pathology leads to tau pathology” – are not explicitly encoded in the graph at this time. Extracting or encoding such relationships remains challenging. We have a few avenues to pursue:
Incorporating Domain Knowledge: We can integrate known causal links from literature and expert knowledge as additional edges or nodes. For example, adding a conceptual node “Amyloid_Plaques” and linking it to “Tau_Tangles” with a CAUSES edge (based on neuropathological studies)
mdpi.com
 could embed a known causal hypothesis into the graph. Similarly, linking “APOE4” to “Higher Amyloid Levels” could be justified by numerous studies
bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com
. This essentially blends our data-driven graph with a knowledge-driven graph. The risk is that adding too many assumed links might bias analyses, so it must be done carefully and perhaps with provenance (so we know which links are data-derived vs. assumed).
Causal Discovery Algorithms: We intend to apply algorithms that infer causal structure from data, such as constraint-based methods (PC algorithm) or score-based methods (Bayesian network structure learning), on the data extracted from the graph. Because our data is longitudinal, we can leverage temporal causal discovery (e.g., Granger causality, or structural causal models that incorporate time). The knowledge graph simplifies the extraction of the variables needed for these algorithms – for instance, we can easily pull time series of cognitive scores and imaging biomarkers for each patient. A near-term step is to create a simplified dataset of key features (like a table of potential causes and effects over time) from the graph and feed it to such algorithms. The expectation is to learn a causal graph (perhaps smaller and more abstract than the full knowledge graph) that might tell us, for example, that baseline amyloid influences future tau and volume loss, which in turn influence cognitive decline (a chain aligning with the amyloid cascade hypothesis), while accounting for genetic predispositions as confounders
mdpi.com
.
Causal Querying and Reasoning: Another direction is to use the graph directly in causal reasoning frameworks, such as do-calculus queries. Recent work like Callahan et al.’s shows that one can use a knowledge graph to identify mediators/confounders
mdpi.com
. We could implement a form of causal query where a user asks, “Given this graph, what nodes could explain the association between X and Y?” The system would then search for common ancestors or intermediate paths between X and Y in the knowledge graph. This is an AI problem that intersects graph algorithms and causal inference. Our current graph would likely yield many possible paths (some spurious), but by overlaying statistical strength information (e.g., weighting edges by correlation strength observed in data), we could rank the plausible paths. This concept moves toward a causal knowledge graph, where edges might carry a weight or label indicating causal effect size or confidence.
Current Graph Use and Users: At present, our knowledge graph serves as a powerful integrative tool for researchers familiar with the data. However, making it broadly useful will require user-friendly interfaces and possibly incorporating it into workflows of clinicians or data scientists. One discussion point is how to enable querying by domain experts who are not graph experts. We found Cypher to be expressive but it has a learning curve. In the future, we could implement a question-answer interface (potentially with natural language queries) that translates questions into graph queries – e.g. “Find patients with X and Y who developed Z” could be interpreted and executed on the graph. Another angle is visualization tools: while Neo4j Bloom is helpful, more advanced visualization focusing on temporal biomedical data might be needed to truly convey the information (for instance, an interactive timeline that is backed by the graph queries).
Interoperability and Standards: We aligned our graph with an ontology (AD-DPC) and could further align it with standard schemas (RDF or others) to facilitate data sharing. There’s a trade-off between the efficiency of a property graph and the interoperability of semantic web formats (like RDF triples). We chose Neo4j property graph for ease and speed, but in the future, we might export a version of the data as RDF to be compatible with other knowledge bases or to leverage semantic reasoners. This could also allow linking with projects like the Linked Open Data cloud or specific resources like DBpedia for drug information, etc.
In reflection, building this AD knowledge graph has illuminated how complex medical data can benefit from graph-based representation, aligning with observations in the broader graph community that highly connected, heterogeneous data is often more naturally handled in graph databases than in traditional relational databases
neo4j.com
neo4j.com
. The success of our initial experiments encourages us that continuing down this path – augmenting the graph with more data (other cohorts, or post-mortem data, etc.), more knowledge (pathway databases, literature mining), and analytic layers (causal inference, predictive modeling) – could yield a powerful platform for AD research. Ultimately, our vision is that such a knowledge graph could enable researchers to seamlessly traverse from genes to brain scans to clinical outcomes, ask “what if” questions (simulating interventions by removing or modifying certain edges/nodes), and perhaps generate hypotheses for clinical trials (e.g., identifying a subgroup of patients who might respond to a particular therapy based on a causal chain discovered in the graph). Future Directions: Concretely, the next steps we plan include:
Formal Causal Modeling: Using the graph data to build a dynamic Bayesian network or structural equation model that can be tested against longitudinal outcomes. This might involve simplifying the graph to key variables and using something like the do-calculus to estimate causal effects (for example, the causal effect of lowering amyloid on cognitive decline, using graph-derived data
mdpi.com
).
Integration of Multi-cohort Data: ADNI is just one study. We could integrate data from other studies (e.g. AIBL, UK Biobank, or AMP-AD which has a lot of -omics data) into the graph. This would dramatically increase the scale, but also the richness. Challenges there include harmonizing different data schemas and dealing with any batch effects or differences in population.
Knowledge Graph Embeddings and ML: We can compute embeddings of the knowledge graph (transforming each node into a vector such that graph relationships are preserved) using techniques like GraphSAGE or TransE. These embeddings could feed into machine learning models to predict outcomes (similar to what Yang et al. did with ADKG for predictive modeling
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
). For example, each patient node’s embedding might encapsulate their entire data and could be used to predict progression to AD. We foresee trying such approaches and comparing them to classical models that use tabular features.
Collaboration and Crowdsourcing: Making the graph available to the wider community (assuming data use agreements permit) could allow other researchers to contribute. For instance, someone might add a new relationship or a new set of nodes (like adding a set of drug treatment nodes for patients who were on certain medications). Crowdsourcing in a knowledge graph is tricky (quality control is needed), but we might manage contributions via a curated process.
Front-end Application: To truly leverage the graph, a front-end application for clinicians or researchers could be built. Imagine a clinician querying the graph for a patient’s data during an assessment, or a researcher dragging and dropping variables to see connections. This would involve engineering beyond the scope of this paper, but our current results form the core backend for such possibilities.
In closing, this project demonstrates an important point: big data in Alzheimer’s disease can be harnessed more effectively when its inherent relationships are made explicit. By transforming the ADNI dataset into a network of meaningful connections, we have taken a step toward not just cataloguing data, but enabling reasoning over it. The knowledge graph approach has provided clarity (short queries replacing manual searches through dozens of files) and opened the door for advanced analyses. There are still significant hurdles to making this a fully realized “causal knowledge graph” – causation is the holy grail that requires careful methodological advances – but the work so far provides a strong foundation. We have at our disposal a comprehensive digital model of a large AD cohort, and with continued development, we hope this model will yield new insights into the causal mechanisms of Alzheimer’s disease and ultimately guide more effective interventions for this devastating condition.
