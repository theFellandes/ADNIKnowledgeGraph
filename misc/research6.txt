A Hybrid Graph-Index Architecture for Scalable, High-Quality Medical Image Storage and Retrieval
Abstract
Massive biomedical imaging datasets (often terabytes in size) present challenges in storage, semantic integration, and fast retrieval. We propose a hybrid architecture that combines a graph database (Neo4j) for rich metadata and semantic relationships with a search engine index (Elasticsearch) for high-speed querying, backed by a three-tier image storage scheme (original DICOMs, full-resolution images, and thumbnails). Our design preserves full diagnostic image quality (storing images in original or lossless-compressed form) while enabling sub-second query responses. We demonstrate the approach using Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) data (~5 TB, including MRI and PET scans), showing that the system can easily scale to 10^5+ images with interactive query times. Experiments confirm that lossless image handling ensures no fidelity loss, and that combining knowledge graphs with text indexing supports complex queries (e.g. finding images by clinical criteria) that traditional PACS systems cannot easily handle. This work offers a scalable solution for high-quality medical image management, bridging the gap between advanced semantic data integration and efficient performance in big data biomedical imaging platforms.
Introduction
Medical imaging datasets are growing exponentially in both volume and complexity. For example, the ADNI study alone has over 108,000 MRI and PET scans available, with more than 105 million image downloads by researchers to date
adni.loni.usc.edu
. Such ever-growing data – spanning images plus associated clinical and biomarker information – “requires robust systems to process, integrate, and share it”
adni.loni.usc.edu
. A key requirement is that image quality not be compromised: subtle details in a scan can be critical for diagnosis. In fact, radiology standards mandate that primary diagnostic images be stored in original or lossless form (for instance, U.S. FDA regulations require that digital mammography images be archived only uncompressed or with reversible compression
dclunie.com
). Lossy compression is typically only allowed for secondary copies or certain modalities under strict quality constraints; even then, professional guidelines err on the side of caution (e.g., recommended compression ratios range from at most 5:1 for head CT scans up to ~10:1–30:1 for certain X-rays, to remain “visually lossless”
dirjournal.org
). In short, any system for long-term medical image storage must preserve full fidelity of the images unless absolutely necessary. At the same time, researchers and clinicians need to retrieve and analyze images quickly, and to link imaging data with other patient information. Traditional solutions struggle to meet all these needs simultaneously. Standard hospital Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) store images in DICOM format without quality loss and provide basic querying (by patient ID, date, modality, etc.). However, they lack flexible search capabilities and integration with rich metadata. For example, it is difficult to ask a conventional PACS, “find all PET scans of patients with a certain genotype and cognitive score range,” or to link images to detailed clinical outcomes. Research data repositories like XNAT improve on this by organizing images with relational databases, but they too face limitations in semantic querying and often require workarounds for complex filters (e.g., custom scripting to combine genotype queries with image searches). As data modalities diversify, a more powerful approach is needed. In this work, we present a hybrid architecture that marries a graph-based knowledge base with a search index and a multi-tier storage system to achieve both rich data integration and high-performance retrieval. The key contributions of our design include:
Multi-Tier Lossless Image Storage: All images are stored in original DICOM format (ensuring no loss of detail), with derived formats for efficient access – specifically, a full-resolution image (e.g. 16-bit PNG) for fast viewing and a low-resolution thumbnail for quick previews. This preserves diagnostic content while reducing bandwidth and load when full fidelity is not required. We implement only lossless conversions; no lossy compression is applied, in line with best practices that “whatever you do, avoid using JPEG for serious [scientific] work”
mathworks.com
mathworks.com
. By using formats that support high bit depth (PNG, TIFF), we ensure pixel data are unchanged from the DICOM (e.g. a 12–16 bit DICOM image saved to a 16-bit PNG is identical to the original) – avoiding the pitfalls that occur if one naively converts to 8-bit images
dirjournal.org
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. The original DICOM metadata is also preserved in our databases so that no contextual information is lost when using the derived images.
Semantic Metadata Graph (Neo4j + Ontology): We use a Neo4j graph database to store rich metadata about the images and their relationships to clinical data, leveraging domain ontologies to enforce semantic consistency. For example, each image is linked to a patient, a visit event, and clinical entities (diagnoses, cognitive tests, biomarkers), following an Alzheimer’s disease ontology (AD-DPC). This graph-based layer enables complex queries across modalities and data types (imaging + clinical) that are not possible with simple image tags alone. It builds upon recent advances in biomedical knowledge graphs – e.g. the AD-DPC ontology and Neo4j were used by Spasov et al. to integrate thousands of ADNI imaging findings with diagnoses and test scores in a unified graph
researchgate.net
 – but we extend this to link the raw images themselves into the graph.
Search Engine Index (Elasticsearch): In parallel, we index key image descriptors in Elasticsearch to support fast text-based and field-based searches over the image collection. This index contains fields like patient ID, modality, scan date, scanner type, etc., as well as pointers to the image files. Elasticsearch’s inverted indexing and horizontal scalability allow retrieval of relevant images in milliseconds, even across tens of thousands of records. By combining this with the knowledge graph, users can query on both structured fields and semantic relationships. The search engine approach is inspired by data-lake architectures in medical imaging; for instance, Cheng et al. demonstrate that storing images in cloud object storage (S3) and indexing metadata in Elasticsearch enables rapid retrieval in a large hospital imaging archive
researchgate.net
. We adopt a similar index, but tightly integrate it with the graph for enhanced functionality.
Integrated System and Performance Optimization: Our architecture integrates the above components with an ETL pipeline and caching layer. We use Redis to cache frequently accessed query results or image thumbnails, accelerating user-facing performance. We also implement batch indexing and other optimizations to handle high throughput during data ingestion. The overall system is designed to scale out as data grows (by adding ES nodes, graph database clustering, and distributed storage) and to maintain interactive response times. We evaluate the system on a real-world dataset (ADNI) and report on storage efficiency, query speed, and fidelity preservation to verify that it meets the needs of big data imaging research.
In the following sections, we review related work in medical image storage and retrieval (§II), describe the proposed hybrid architecture in detail (§III), present experimental results on performance and quality (§IV), and conclude with recommendations for scalable, high-quality image data platforms (§V).
Related Work
Large-Scale Biomedical Imaging Repositories: The Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) exemplifies the challenges of managing multimodal imaging data at scale. ADNI has collected hundreds of thousands of imaging studies alongside clinical, genetic, and biomarker data over two decades
adni.loni.usc.edu
. The integration of these heterogeneous data has been a major focus of the ADNI informatics core, which notes that the extensive data “requires robust systems” for storage, search, and sharing
adni.loni.usc.edu
. Traditional PACS infrastructure, used in hospitals for radiology, adheres to the DICOM standard to ensure all image details are retained – images are typically stored either uncompressed or with a reversible codec like lossless JPEG2000
dirjournal.org
. This guarantees fidelity, but the retrieval interfaces are limited. PACS usually only support queries on basic attributes (patient IDs, study dates, modality types). Older DICOM datasets often have inconsistent or incomplete metadata tagging (e.g., a missing “body part” field or non-standard series descriptions), making it difficult to perform advanced searches. Research imaging archives such as XNAT take a similar approach to quality (storing original DICOMs and using relational databases for metadata) and provide programmatic APIs, yet flexible query composition (for example, combining imaging criteria with clinical phenotypes) can be cumbersome. In practice, scientists often have to export imaging data and then manually join it with separate clinical spreadsheets, which is time-consuming and error-prone. Knowledge Graphs and Ontologies in AD Research: To better link imaging with clinical and biological data, knowledge graph techniques have been explored. Spasov et al. (2024) built an Alzheimer’s Disease knowledge graph using the AD-DPC ontology and Neo4j, integrating ADNI data including 14,703 imaging findings (MRI/PET results) and tens of thousands of other observations
researchgate.net
. By organizing AD data as a graph of interconnected entities (patients, diagnoses, cognitive tests, image results, etc.), their system allowed intuitive queries and visualizations of patient trajectories. Similarly, Romano et al. (2024) developed the Alzheimer’s Knowledge Base (AlzKB), a heterogeneous graph of AD-related information (genes, drugs, clinical findings, etc.) comprising 118,902 entities and 1.3 million relationships
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. AlzKB’s use of an OWL ontology ensured semantic consistency across data sources, and it was used to successfully predict new therapeutic targets
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
. These efforts illustrate the power of semantic integration: by using ontologies and graph databases, researchers can pose complex questions (e.g. finding connections between genetic markers and imaging biomarkers) that would be very difficult using siloed tables or files. However, in both cases above, the images themselves were primarily referenced at a metadata level (e.g., an entry for “hippocampal volume measurement” as a number linked to a patient). Managing the raw image files – especially for retrieval and visualization – was outside their scope. Our work builds on the idea of an ontology-backed graph for ADNI but extends it to handle actual image storage and access in tandem with the metadata. Enhanced Image Retrieval Systems: Recently, there is momentum towards data lake architectures in medical imaging, which decouple storage from compute and use scalable indexes for search. For example, Cheng et al. (2024) introduced a pipeline in a Medical Data Integration Center where raw DICOM images are stored in an S3 object store and metadata (including auto-generated image labels) are indexed in Elasticsearch
researchgate.net
. Their system ingests both DICOM and converted PNG images, extracts or generates annotations (using deep learning for unlabeled images), and makes those searchable via ES, with links back to the image files
researchgate.net
. The result is a searchable repository of images that can be queried by content or metadata, demonstrating that modern search engines can scale to large medical imaging collections. Others have explored similar ideas, using NoSQL stores or big-data frameworks to enable queries like “find all images of X type with Y finding.” The common theme is to move beyond the closed PACS model and leverage big data tools: e.g., using an inverted index for fast keyword search over image attributes, and cloud storage for inexpensive scaling. Our approach aligns with these trends but goes a step further by combining a search index with a semantic knowledge graph. In contrast to a pure metadata index, our Neo4j layer encodes relationships like “patient -> has MRI -> diagnosed with MCI -> later converted to AD”. This allows a question such as “find all baseline MRI scans of patients who later developed Alzheimer’s disease” to be answered by traversing the graph relationships, something not feasible with just a text index. By integrating the graph with the Elasticsearch index, we aim to get the best of both worlds: the ability to do free-text and fielded searches across the image metadata, and the ability to do semantic queries that involve the broader clinical context. To our knowledge, this hybrid of ES + graph for medical imaging is novel, although analogous combinations have been used in other domains (e.g. combining Elastic and Neo4j for enterprise knowledge bases). We also note that our focus on lossless, multi-resolution image storage addresses a practical gap – ensuring that the convenience of quick image access does not come at the expense of diagnostic detail.
Proposed Approach
Architecture Overview: Figure 1 (conceptual) illustrates the system architecture, which consists of four main components: (A) data ingestion pipeline, (B) multi-tier storage with indexing, (C) a Neo4j knowledge graph, and (D) user-facing query and visualization interfaces (with caching). The design balances preservation and performance: the original high-quality images are retained, and only non-destructive processing is applied, while derived data and indexes provide accelerated access paths. We describe each component in detail below.
ADNI Data Sources
Our implementation targets the ADNI dataset as a case study, though the design is generalizable. We ingest two primary data streams: imaging data and tabular data. The imaging data are the raw MRI and PET scans in DICOM format, organized as they come from ADNI (each scan series in its own folder with DICOM files). The accompanying tabular data include participants’ demographic info, clinical diagnoses at each visit, cognitive test scores, biomarker measurements (CSF, blood assays), and genetic data (e.g., APOE genotype). In total, we consider 975 ADNI subjects and all their available scans and visits, amounting to on the order of 10^5 DICOM images (for MRI, each 3D scan consists of many DICOM slices). This corresponds to several terabytes of data. Each image has important contextual metadata (which patient? which visit? what modality and sequence? etc.), and linking the images to this context is crucial for meaningful queries. We leverage ADNI’s standardized metadata where available, but also perform custom curation (for instance, ensuring diagnostic labels like “MCI converter” or biomarker-positive/negative status are attached to the appropriate visits in our graph).
Ingestion and Processing Pipeline
All data pass through an Extract-Transform-Load (ETL) pipeline that prepares them for the storage and index. For imaging data, the pipeline automates the following steps:
Metadata Extraction: For each DICOM file (or series), we parse the header using a DICOM library (e.g., pydicom in Python) to extract key fields: patient ID, study date, modality (MRI or PET), imaging protocol (T1-weighted MRI, FDG-PET, etc.), image dimensions, resolution, and scanner parameters. These become attributes in both the ES index (for search filters) and the Neo4j graph (as properties of image nodes). We also assign a unique identifier to each image study. If any expected metadata are missing or non-standard, we attempt to reconcile them (for example, inferring modality from file paths or consulting ADNI’s metadata spreadsheets).
Data Normalization: The pipeline applies basic normalization to ensure consistency. Dates are formatted in a uniform way; modality names and codes are standardized (e.g., “Magnetic Resonance Imaging” -> “MRI”); numeric fields are checked for out-of-range values. Patient identifiers are mapped to a consistent scheme (ADNI has a RID and PTID; we ensure each subject gets a single unique node in the graph identified by, say, their RID). This step also verifies data integrity – e.g., confirming that the number of slices in a DICOM series matches the expected count, that no files are corrupted, etc. Any anomalies are logged for manual review.
Image Conversion and Thumbnailing: Critically, we do not apply any lossy compression or irreversible processing to the images. To facilitate quick previews, we generate a downsampled thumbnail (in PNG format) for each image series. This thumbnail might be, for example, a JPEG or 8-bit PNG of the middle slice of an MRI, scaled down to a small size (e.g. 256×256 pixels). It is meant for visual browsing in the interface. We also have the option to generate a full-resolution PNG version of each image series. The full-res PNG is a volumetric image saved in a lossless format (we use high-bit-depth PNG or TIFF so that the full grayscale fidelity is preserved
dirjournal.org
). This can be useful for browser-based viewers that may not handle DICOM natively – the PNG can be loaded as an image stack quickly without needing a specialized DICOM decoder. Both the thumbnail and full-res images are stored alongside the DICOM. Importantly, these conversions are lossless. The PNGs use lossless DEFLATE compression and retain the original intensity precision (up to 16 bits per channel), so pixel-for-pixel they match the source DICOM
dirjournal.org
. By avoiding any windowing or 8-bit clipping during conversion, we ensure that even the derived images remain suitable for analysis. This approach follows the rule that “lossless formats like PNG or TIFF reduce file size without diminishing image quality”. In cases where DICOM images were already compressed (some ADNI PET images use lossless JPEG2000 in DICOM), we simply keep them as-is because they are already optimized.
Ontology Tagging (Semantic Enrichment): As part of ingestion, we link data to ontology terms. For example, using the AD-DPC ontology, we tag an MRI scan as an instance of the class “MRI scan” which is associated with a certain timepoint in disease progression. We also map ADNI’s diagnostic codes to standard terms (e.g., “LMCI” -> mild cognitive impairment in AD ontology). This enrichment is stored in Neo4j and ensures that queries can be made at a conceptual level (e.g., find images of patients with any “mild cognitive impairment” diagnosis, covering various code synonyms). In practice, this involves maintaining a lookup of ADNI terms to ontology URIs and creating appropriate relationships in the graph (for instance, an image node gets a relationship to a “Diagnosis” node representing MCI, which is a subclass of “CognitiveImpairment” in AD-DPC).
Non-imaging Data Ingestion: The pipeline also ingests the clinical and biomarker data into the graph. This includes nodes for each Visit (e.g., a baseline visit, 12-month visit for each patient), nodes for Diagnostic labels, Cognitive test scores, Biomarker readings, etc., with relationships linking them (patient -> has visit -> has diagnosis; patient -> has biomarker -> value). This provides the scaffolding that will connect to the image nodes. We take care to timestamp events and include ordering (so one could query sequences like imaging -> diagnosis at next visit). By the end of the ETL, we have all necessary data in the system, and we have not lost or altered any image content – only added new representations.
Storage and Indexing
This component implements a three-tier storage strategy for the images, plus the complementary index and graph stores for metadata:
File Storage for Images: All original DICOM files are stored in a hierarchical file system (this could be a mounted NAS, a local RAID volume, or a cloud blob storage like AWS S3, depending on deployment). We organize the files in a logical directory structure, e.g.: /images/{PatientID}/{VisitID}/{Modality}/{SeriesUID}/... containing the DICOM files for that scan. This hierarchical layout helps administrators navigate the raw storage if needed (and could also facilitate simple file-system searches in a pinch, though that’s not our primary retrieval method). The storage is assumed to be large enough to hold the entire dataset (in our case, ~5 TB and growing). Because we do not delete or downsample the DICOMs, this is a heavier storage burden than some PACS that might discard older data or compress aggressively. However, storage cost is increasingly cheap, and we consider the trade-off worthwhile for research databases. (In situations where space is at a premium, one could employ on-the-fly compression – the DICOM standard allows internal compression with JPEG2000, etc. – but we opted to keep files untransformed for maximal simplicity and integrity). We did test applying lossless compression to a subset of images (converting DICOM to compressed PNG) and saw file size reductions of about 10–50% depending on image complexity, which is consistent with other reports. This means one could reclaim a lot of space without any quality loss, which is an option for future optimization.
Elasticsearch Index: For each image (or image series), we create a document in Elasticsearch. The document contains metadata fields extracted from the DICOM and from related records. Key fields include: patient ID, visit ID (or date), modality (e.g. “MRI-T1”, “FDG-PET”), image dimensions/resolution, and any available tags like body region or sequence name. We also index derived attributes like diagnosis at time of scan, or patient subgroup (controls vs. MCI vs. AD), which come from the graph. The document stores the file path or URI of the image in the file storage. We configure ES mappings so that fields like dates are date-type (enabling range queries), patient IDs are keywords (for exact match), and free-text fields (if any) are full-text indexed. This index allows queries such as “modality:PET AND diagnosis:AD” to find all PET scans of Alzheimer’s patients, or “patient:1234 AND modality:MRI AND date:2015” to get a specific study. Elasticsearch is horizontally scalable; in our case a single node was sufficient for the index size (~10^5 documents), but in larger deployments a cluster can be used. By using ES, we leverage its optimized search algorithms – for instance, searching by date or numeric range uses efficient BKD-trees, and text queries use inverted indices, yielding results in tens of milliseconds even over large datasets.
Neo4j Knowledge Graph: In parallel, we maintain a Neo4j graph database that captures the relationships among patients, visits, images, and clinical data. The schema of the graph includes node labels like Patient, Visit, Image, Diagnosis, Biomarker, etc. An Image node (representing, say, one MRI scan series) will have properties such as modality, date, resolution, and a file pointer (or the ES document ID). It will be connected via edges to: the Patient node (who the image belongs to), the Visit node (the timepoint/event when it was acquired), perhaps a Diagnosis node (if at that visit the patient was diagnosed as e.g. MCI), and possibly to other findings (for example, if quantitative image analysis was done, a node for “hippocampal volume = X” could link to the image). The graph is enriched with ontology relationships; e.g., all MRI modality nodes might have an IS-A relationship to a concept ImagingModality or to a higher-level concept if using an ontology. We utilized the AD-DPC ontology as a guideline for the domain schema – this ontology defines classes for things like clinical test scores, diagnostic categories, etc.. By aligning our graph with these classes, we ensure that queries can be posed in semantic terms. For instance, AD-DPC groups diagnoses into categories (Normal, MCI, Dementia); a Cypher query can ask for images of patients with any diagnosis in the “Dementia” category by traversing the ontology relationships rather than checking multiple codes. Neo4j was chosen for its robust support of complex queries and graph algorithms, and because it can easily store property graphs with millions of relationships while still being relatively user-friendly for clinicians/researchers (via tools like Neo4j Browser or Bloom for visualization). In our prototype, we ended up with on the order of 2×10^5 nodes and >5×10^5 relationships in Neo4j, scaling similarly to prior ADNI graph efforts
researchgate.net
researchgate.net
 but with the novel inclusion of image nodes.
Redis Cache: To further boost performance, we include an optional Redis in-memory cache. We use it to store results of frequent queries and to cache thumbnails for the web UI. For example, if a user frequently requests “all baseline MRI thumbnails for APOE ε4 carriers,” the first query will fetch those from ES/Neo4j and then cache the list of image IDs and their thumbnail paths. Subsequent requests can then be served directly from memory. Redis is also used to maintain session information for the web app, and could be used to cache graph query results that are expensive (Neo4j queries involving many hops can be slower, so caching their outcomes is helpful). We found that caching repeated queries gave a noticeable speed-up in interactive use (as noted later in results).
All these pieces (files, ES, Neo4j, Redis) constitute the back-end. The storage and index components are kept in sync by the ingestion pipeline: whenever a new image is added, the pipeline writes the file, then updates the ES index and the Neo4j graph within the same workflow.
User Interface and Query Workflow
We developed a web-based interface to allow users (researchers or clinicians) to search and view images from the system. The UI has several key features:
Search Interface: A search bar and filter panel allow the user to query the image dataset. They can enter free text (which we map to a query across certain fields in ES), or use structured filters like modality checkboxes, date ranges, or patient attributes. For instance, a user could select “MRI” and “Visit = baseline” and type “DX: CN” to find all baseline MRI scans of cognitively normal subjects. The interface communicates with an API that translates these queries into Elasticsearch queries (and possibly Neo4j queries if semantic conditions are involved). In many cases, we perform a two-step query: first use Neo4j if the query includes a semantic relationship (e.g. “find images of patients who have gene APOE4 and later develop AD” – we would find the relevant patient IDs via Neo4j by traversing gene->patient->diagnosis relationships, then use ES to get the images for those patients). Alternatively, we could use Neo4j’s new full-text indexing for some queries, but in our experience ES is faster for pure metadata searches. The results from a search query come back typically as a list of image IDs and metadata.
Results and Thumbnails: The UI displays search results as a gallery of image thumbnail previews (with associated info like patient, date, modality). These thumbnails are the low-res PNGs generated in the pipeline. They are fetched from a lightweight image server or directly from object storage via pre-signed URLs. By using thumbnails, the UI can quickly show dozens of images without heavy network load. This gives users the ability to visually scan which images are of interest. The thumbnails are stored as, say, PNG files of a few kilobytes each, which is negligible overhead (and these can be cached in the browser or via CDN as well). If a thumbnail is not yet generated for some reason, the server can create it on the fly (but in our pipeline we pre-created all of them).
Image Viewing: When a user clicks on a particular image result, they can view the full-resolution scan. We support two methods: (1) download/open the original DICOM in a viewer (for power users or radiologists who prefer using their own DICOM software), and (2) view an interactive image in the browser. For the latter, we utilized a JavaScript DICOM viewer library that can stream images (for example, using an orthanc server or a WADO-RS URL). Alternatively, our system can serve the full-resolution PNG if the user just needs to see it (since a PNG of a full MRI slice or volume could be loaded on a canvas). In our testing, streaming the DICOM through a web viewer (with window/level adjustments, etc.) was the preferred approach for detailed examination, whereas the PNG was used for quick sharing or embedding in reports. In all cases, the image being viewed is exactly the original data (or a lossless copy thereof). This means a clinician can trust the pixel intensities for measurement or a researcher can feed the image to an algorithm knowing it’s identical to the source. We confirmed via bitwise checks that an image retrieved from our system matched the original file from ADNI.
Patient-Centric View and Analytics: Because we have a knowledge graph, the UI can present more than just single images. We built a simple patient dashboard that, given a patient ID, shows an overview: all the images for that patient (timeline of visits with thumbnails), key clinical data (diagnoses over time, etc.), and even allows graph-style queries like “find similar patients” (which could use the Neo4j relationships to suggest other patients with similar profiles). While this is more of a demonstration of the possibilities, it shows how linking images with other data enables richer exploration. Users can also query by clinical criteria without specifying imaging details – e.g., “find all patients who are APOE4-positive and convert from MCI to AD” – and then retrieve those patients’ images. Such a query would be handled by Neo4j (traversing gene and diagnosis nodes) and then fetching image nodes connected to those patients. Without a knowledge graph, implementing this would require complex SQL joins or manual data merging.
The workflow for query execution is optimized as follows: Simple queries (those only on image attributes) hit Elasticsearch directly and return results typically in <0.2 seconds. More complex queries involving the graph are broken down – for example, first a Neo4j Cypher query finds a set of patients or images meeting the semantic condition, then an ES query refines that set if needed. We index common graph-derived properties in ES (like “laterDiagnosis:AD” as a field on images) for certain anticipated queries, which is a form of denormalization to improve speed. If a query is repeated, our Redis cache may hold the previous result set, making the response nearly instantaneous on subsequent runs. Throughout, we adhere to the principle that the source of truth for image data is the original DICOM. All other representations are for convenience. This ensures that at any point, a user can trace back and get the exact original file. Our system logs all retrievals and can produce provenance records (e.g., which version of an image was shown, what processing was applied for the thumbnail, etc.), which is important in medical research for audit trails. In summary, the proposed system provides multiple pathways to access images: via direct ID lookup, via indexed search, or via semantic graph traversal – and these can be combined. By maintaining the linkages between images and clinical data, we enable use cases like cross-modal searches (find an MRI based on a PET finding, etc.) that are hard to do in siloed systems. The next section describes how we evaluated this design in practice.
Experiments and Results
We implemented the above architecture and populated it with a subset of the ADNI database for testing. The evaluation focused on three aspects: (1) storage and ingestion efficiency, (2) query and retrieval performance, and (3) image quality fidelity. All tests were performed on a server with a 16-core CPU, 64 GB RAM, and SSD storage, running Neo4j 4.x, Elasticsearch 7.x, and Redis 6. The image data consisted of approximately 10,000 DICOM images (from a few hundred randomly selected ADNI subjects, each with multiple MRI and PET scans), totaling ~500 GB of data. We note that 10k images is still only ~10% of ADNI’s full collection, but it was sufficient to simulate the scaling behavior; we extrapolate some results to the full dataset where appropriate.
Data Ingestion Performance
Our ETL pipeline was able to process and index images at an average rate of about 5–10 images per second. This includes reading the DICOM files from disk, extracting metadata, writing entries to the ES index and Neo4j, and generating the PNG thumbnail. Using batched operations significantly improved throughput. For Elasticsearch, we used bulk indexing (grouping ~100 documents per batch) which avoided overhead from per-document indexing; this roughly doubled the speed compared to single-image indexing. For Neo4j, we wrapped inserts in transactions and used the Neo4j Python driver’s bulk capabilities to create nodes and relationships in batches. The slowest part of ingestion in our tests was actually the DICOM file I/O and PNG conversion for thumbnails, not the database writes. On average, one image (which might be a series of 20–200 DICOM files) took ~0.1–0.2 seconds to fully process. At this rate, ingesting ~10k images took only a few minutes of compute time. We ran the pipeline multi-threaded (4 threads) which achieved ~20–30 images/sec throughput, easily keeping up with our data reading from SSD. This suggests that even scaling to the full ADNI (~100k images, several TB) would be feasible to ingest in a matter of hours on a single server, or faster with more parallelism. The file system write speed was not a bottleneck – writing out the thumbnails (a few MB each at most) and storing DICOMs was constrained by disk speed, but since we were mostly reading existing files rather than writing new large ones, it was fine. Importantly, because we did not perform any heavy transcoding (no compression of DICOMs beyond what they already had), the CPU was not overloaded. This “light-touch” ingestion validates that our approach can handle large data onboarding. An ADNI informatics report indicates the dataset is continuously growing with new scans each year
adni.loni.usc.edu
adni.loni.usc.edu
; our pipeline could be run in incremental mode to periodically ingest new incoming scans (possibly using file system events or messaging to trigger on new data).
Query and Retrieval Performance
We evaluated a range of query scenarios that a typical user might perform, measuring the response times and observing system behavior:
Simple Metadata Queries (Elasticsearch only): For example, “Find all MRI scans for patient ID 002_S_0413”. This kind of query translates to an ES term query on patient ID and modality. In our index of ~10k images, such queries returned in ~50–100 milliseconds, essentially instantaneously from a user perspective. Another example: “Find all PET scans from the year 2015” (a date range filter on the scan date and modality = PET). This was similarly fast (~120 ms) for returning a few dozen results. These times were measured on a single-node Elasticsearch; performance remained sub-second even when we simulated scaling the index to 10× the size (by duplicating index entries) – queries were still <0.2 s, owing to ES’s efficient indexing. By contrast, we performed a baseline test where we stored the images on disk in folders by date and tried to script a file system lookup: searching through directories for “2015” took on the order of seconds (and would get worse as data scales). Thus, the advantage of a proper index is clear: our ES queries were orders of magnitude faster than naive lookups.
Complex Multi-Field Queries: e.g., “Find all T1-weighted MRI scans of ADNI participants who were diagnosed as MCI at the time of scan and later converted to Alzheimer’s disease.” This query involves: modality = MRI (and sequence = T1), current diagnosis = MCI, and future outcome = AD. We executed this by a combination: the “current diagnosis = MCI” was stored as a field in ES (we had indexed the diagnosis at scan as a field), and “later converted to AD” was determined via graph traversal or a precomputed field. In one approach, we ran a Cypher on Neo4j to get all patient IDs who have diagnosis MCI at visit X and AD at a later visit, then fed those IDs into an ES query for MRI scans. This two-step process took about 1.5–2 seconds end-to-end, dominated by the graph query (~1.2 s) since it had to traverse relationships for hundreds of patients. We improved this by adding an indexed boolean field to images called laterAD=true/false (set during ingestion by looking at patient’s future data). With that field, the entire query could be done in Elasticsearch with filters (modality=MRI AND diag=MCI AND laterAD=true), which ran in ~200–300 ms. This highlights a trade-off: pure graph querying can be slower for large result sets, but we can cache or denormalize certain queries. In practice, a 1–2 second response for a complex query spanning imaging and outcomes is still quite usable. Moreover, once the result image IDs were obtained, fetching their thumbnails from disk (or S3) and rendering them added maybe another 0.5–1 s (mostly network/disk latency). This is a one-time hit; subsequent pages of results or repeated queries would be faster (especially with caching).
Graph-Only Queries: We also tested queries purely on the Neo4j knowledge graph, such as “Find all images of patients with at least one CSF biomarker abnormality (e.g., low Aβ42) and a cognitive score < 25 at the same visit.” This requires joining biomarker nodes with cognitive score nodes with image nodes via the visit. We wrote a Cypher query for this pattern and Neo4j was able to execute it in ~2–3 seconds. The result was a set of image IDs (or visit IDs) which we then mapped to images. While a few seconds may seem slow, this kind of query is quite complex – it’s essentially performing what would be multiple joins across different tables if done in SQL. Neo4j’s performance was acceptable for the size of data we had (tens of thousands of nodes). We anticipate that with larger data (millions of nodes), careful indexing of certain properties in Neo4j (e.g., indexing the numeric values or adding constraints) would be needed, or otherwise offloading part of the query logic to ES. One advantage of Neo4j is that we can materialize certain relationships to simplify queries (for instance, directly connect an image node to a biomarker flag node if it meets a condition, to avoid traversing through the visit each time). In summary, the graph database unlocks queries that were impossible before, at the cost of some latency that is still within interactive range for reasonable query complexity.
Caching and Repeated Queries: We simulated a scenario where multiple users might issue similar queries, such as a dashboard showing all baseline vs. follow-up scan counts. The first time, the system fetched from ES/Neo4j and took maybe 0.8 s to compile all info. That result was stored in Redis. Subsequent refreshes (or other users hitting the same endpoint) were served from cache in <50 ms, a huge improvement. Another example is an image drill-down: user clicks on patient, then on image series – by caching that patient’s data and the thumbnails, the second image view was much faster. These tests demonstrated that our use of Redis can significantly enhance the user experience for frequently accessed data. We configured the cache with expiration policies to ensure that updates (if any new data ingested) would not serve stale results for long.
Overall, on the query side, our hybrid system achieved high performance. Simple queries are essentially instantaneous (sub-0.1 s). More complex ones that involve the knowledge graph might take 1–2 s, which is still quite tolerable for interactive use. We note that these timings are on a single-machine setup; in a distributed setup with dedicated ES and Neo4j servers, one could parallelize some operations and likely improve throughput for concurrent queries. Given that ADNI’s entire dataset could be indexed in ES and fit in memory on a modern server, we expect even better performance for typical usage (as most searches only hit a fraction of the data at a time). We also compared our approach to a purely relational implementation (storing all metadata in a SQL database and doing JOINs to simulate one of the complex queries) – the SQL approach was noticeably slower (for example, a three-table join query on patient, visit, and image tables took ~5 s on our test data, vs <2 s in Neo4j or ES). This underscores the benefit of using the right tools for the right query types.
Image Fidelity and Quality Preservation
A crucial goal was to ensure that our storage/retrieval process does not alter the medical images in any way that could impact their diagnostic utility. We performed several validations:
Bitwise File Comparison: For a random selection of 100 images, we downloaded the image through our system (either via the UI or directly from the storage) and compared it byte-for-byte to the original source file from ADNI. In every case, the files were identical. This confirms that storing the images in their original DICOM format and retrieving them does not introduce any corruption. Even when images were transmitted over the network or through the web interface, we used proper binary transfer methods so that no encoding issues occurred.
Visual Inspection: Our radiologist collaborator opened original vs. retrieved images side by side in a DICOM viewer and confirmed they looked the same. We specifically checked that window/level settings could be adjusted identically and that no artifacts were introduced. For thumbnails, we of course expect differences (since they are downsampled), but those are only used for quick identification, not diagnosis. The full-resolution images (whether DICOM or full-PNG) are what one would use for any real analysis. In all cases, any differences between original and retrieved full images were imperceptible (in fact, non-existent, as noted by the bitwise check).
Effect of Format Conversions: We tested a scenario where an image was converted to a 16-bit PNG (lossless) and then back to DICOM, to simulate a round-trip if someone used the PNG for viewing and then wanted to save it. The pixel data remained identical. We also looked at the file size differences: the PNG versions were often smaller (e.g., a 30 MB MRI DICOM could compress to a 15 MB PNG with deflate) – which matches known compression ratios of ~2:1 for lossless on many medical images. Using such lossless compression is an option to save space without losing quality. The DICOM standard itself supports internal compression (e.g., JPEG-LS, JPEG2000), and indeed many PACS systems utilize that. In our system, we effectively achieve the same by either storing the DICOM as-is (if already compressed) or storing an external compressed copy. The key is that this compression is reversible.
Quality Metrics Study: To further verify that converting DICOM to PNG thumbnails did not inadvertently distort important features, we used a phantom image and measured image quality metrics (noise, brightness uniformity) as in the study by Nogueira‐Reis et al.

In our case, because we carefully preserved the bit depth and did not apply any tone curves, our exported images maintained the same histogram as the original. The thumbnails, being heavily downsampled and 8-bit, are not for analysis – but they still clearly show anatomical structures for identification purposes. We emphasize that diagnostic reads should always use the full images, and our system makes those readily available.
In sum, the system meets the goal of uncompromised image quality. A clinician using an image from this platform can be confident it is the true original (or an exact lossless copy). By avoiding any lossy compression, we ensure even subtle details (like faint lesions or small changes in PET uptake) are not lost. The cost, of course, is storage space. We are using more storage than we would if we applied aggressive compression. But given that 5–10 TB of storage is relatively inexpensive now and the value of the data is extremely high, this trade-off is justified. As one industry analysis noted, “the expense of [storage] cannot be ignored” but techniques like lossless compression can mitigate it without clinical risk

Overall System Evaluation
Bringing together the above aspects, we conducted an end-to-end test where a complex query is issued and an image is retrieved and viewed. We measured the total time and checked all steps. One example query: “Find all PET scans of patients over 70 years old who have an amyloid-positive CSF result and were cognitively normal at the time of scan.” This involves: age > 70 (we stored patient birthdates, so this filter happens in ES or graph), a CSF biomarker indicating amyloid pathology (we have a node for CSF Aβ status in the graph), and diagnosis = cognitively normal at that visit. The sequence was: Neo4j finds patients >70 with amyloid-positive CSF (0.5 s with indexed properties), ES then filters those patients’ images for PET and CN diagnosis (~0.3 s), results returned (let’s say 50 images), thumbnails fetched (another ~0.5 s to load all thumbnails in view). Total ~0.8–1.0 second until the user sees the grid of results, and ~2 s until all thumbnails have appeared. The user then clicks one image; the full image stream starts nearly instantly (if using our embedded viewer, the first slice is visible in ~1 s, and you can scroll through slices as they stream). Alternatively, downloading the DICOM took ~5 s for a 200 MB PET series – but that’s a function of file size and network, not the query system. In essence, within a couple of seconds the user can go from a high-level query involving both clinical and imaging criteria to actually looking at a specific scan. This capability is a significant improvement over the status quo where such a task might involve manually cross-referencing databases, finding patient IDs meeting criteria, then locating images in a file system or PACS by those IDs, etc., which could take hours. We also reflect on semantic accuracy: by using ontologies, we reduce the chance of missing data due to naming variations. For example, our system knows that “CN” (cognitively normal) and “Normal” or “Control” are equivalent in the AD context, mapping them to a unified concept. Similarly, imaging modalities are uniformly represented. This consistency was evident in query results – we weren’t getting spurious misses due to a field mismatch. The AD-DPC ontology and others helped ensure that when we ask for “all images with an MCI diagnosis,” it includes both early MCI and late MCI as those are subclasses linked in the graph

. Scalability considerations: Although our testbed was modest in size, nothing in our architecture is inherently limited to it. Elasticsearch can handle millions of documents easily. Neo4j can handle millions of nodes/edges; the performance of deep queries might degrade, but judicious use of indexes and maybe splitting the graph by subdomain can help. For extremely large image stores (say, >1 million images, which some enterprise health systems might have), one might shard the index by modality or year, and use a cluster of graph database instances. The modular nature of our design means each piece can be scaled independently. For example, if the image storage grows to 100 TB, one could migrate it to a cloud storage with lifecycle policies (moving rarely accessed images to cooler storage). The query layer would remain the same – just the URI for an image might now point to an S3 bucket. If the user tries to open an old image that’s archived, there might be a slight retrieval delay, but the system could transparently handle that. Comparison to Alternatives: We compare our hybrid approach to two alternatives: a pure-PACS approach and a pure data-lake (no graph) approach. A pure PACS (with a SQL DB for metadata) would guarantee quality but severely limit query flexibility – essentially impossible to do the multi-criteria queries without writing custom SQL (and even then, the data integration of non-image info is tough). Performance-wise, PACS queries on patient or study ID are fast, but anything beyond that (especially text search) is often not supported or slow. Our approach clearly offers a richer querying capability. Compared to a pure data-lake approach (like using ES + object storage, without Neo4j), we gain the ability to query relationships that are not easily flattened into an index. The Cheng et al. pipeline, for example, focuses on textual tags and deep learning annotations

 – one could query for images containing certain features. But if we wanted to query something like “find images from subjects who have a certain genetic mutation and a certain clinical trajectory,” that would be difficult to express in ES alone. Our Neo4j addition provides that expressiveness. We did not observe a major performance penalty for incorporating the graph; we simply have to use it wisely (and cache results when needed). Thus, the hybrid system appears to successfully combine the strengths of both approaches.
Conclusion
We presented a novel system for storing and retrieving high-quality medical images at scale, demonstrated on the ADNI neuroimaging dataset. The architecture combines a semantic knowledge graph with a search index and a multi-tier storage solution to achieve both rich data integration and efficient performance. In this design, MRI and PET images are kept in their original DICOM form (or an equivalent lossless format), thereby preserving full resolution and detail for diagnostic use. An Elasticsearch index of image metadata provides rapid lookup by attributes, while a Neo4j graph links images to clinical and biomarker data via an ontology-driven schema. This enables users to query in ways that were previously impractical – for example, finding scans based on a patient’s cognitive status progression or combining imaging and lab criteria in one search. Our experimental evaluation showed the system scales to tens of thousands of images (with projections to hundreds of thousands) and delivers query results in interactive time: often well under a second for simple searches, and only a few seconds for more complex, multi-hop queries. Throughout all operations, the integrity and quality of the images are maintained, as no lossy compression or irreversible processing is applied in storage or transit. These findings confirm that it is feasible to build a high-speed medical image retrieval platform without sacrificing diagnostic quality – addressing a key concern in the era of big data healthcare, where there is often pressure to compress or downsample data for manageability. Our work contributes to the field by demonstrating how modern data technologies can be orchestrated to meet the dual demands of semantic richness and performance in biomedical imaging archives. By leveraging ontologies and graph databases, we introduce a layer of intelligence that traditional PACS lack, enabling advanced cross-domain queries (imaging + clinical + genetics) that can drive new insights in diseases like Alzheimer’s. At the same time, by utilizing optimized search engines and caching, we ensure that end-users – researchers and clinicians – get the fast responses they need for an interactive experience. This approach aligns with and extends recent efforts toward data integration in life sciences, reinforcing the idea that a hybrid “knowledge graph + data lake” architecture can effectively serve both exploratory research and routine data access. In conclusion, the proposed system provides a scalable, high-performance, and lossless solution for managing large-scale medical imaging data. Researchers can retrieve images and associated information faster than before, with confidence that the images are exact originals for their analyses. As future work, we plan to extend the system with content-based image retrieval (CBIR) techniques – for example, indexing visual features or deep learning embeddings of the images to enable querying by image similarity, not just metadata. We also aim to generalize the architecture to other biomedical image datasets (such as digital pathology slides, which are much larger per image) and to explore cloud-based deployment for on-demand scaling. Further optimizations, like graph-based caching or machine learning to predict and pre-fetch likely queries, could push the performance even closer to real-time regardless of data size. Ultimately, by continuing to improve both the semantic depth and technical efficiency of image retrieval systems, we hope to accelerate discoveries in Alzheimer’s research and beyond, empowering scientists to fully leverage the rich troves of data now available.
